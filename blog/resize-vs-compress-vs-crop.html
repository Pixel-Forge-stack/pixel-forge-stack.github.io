Resize vs Compress vs Crop: What Each One Does

Three similar-sounding jobs that solve different problems, and the order that usually works best.

People often use "make the image smaller" to mean three different things. Cropping removes part of the picture, resizing changes how many pixels it has, and compressing changes how the file is encoded. Picking the right one saves time and avoids losing quality for nothing.

When to Use Each

Crop when you want a different shape or need to remove something from the frame: a square profile picture, a banner strip, or a tighter composition. Resize when the image has more pixels than you need. A photo that will be shown 800 pixels wide gains little from keeping 4000. Making an image larger does not add detail, so enlarging usually looks soft. Compress when the dimensions are right but the file is too heavy, for example to meet an upload limit. JPG and WebP respond to a quality setting, while PNG does not.

A Sensible Order

For most jobs the order is crop, then resize, then compress.

  1. Crop to the shape you need, so you do not resize pixels you will throw away.
  2. Resize to the dimensions required. This is usually the biggest single saving.
  3. Compress only if the file is still too large, lowering quality gradually and checking the picture.

Example: a phone photo of 4000 × 3000 pixels becomes a profile picture. Crop it to a square around the face, resize it to the size the platform asks for, and compress it only if the upload limit requires it.

Doing the steps in the opposite order wastes effort. Compressing a very large image first, then shrinking it, applies lossy compression twice.

Common Mix-Ups

"Cropping makes the picture smaller." It makes the frame smaller, but the remaining pixels keep their size. Cropping does not scale anything. "Resizing reduces quality." Making an image smaller keeps it looking clean at the new size, though fine detail is lost. Making it larger is the step that makes it look blurry; see why resized images look blurry. "Compressing always shrinks by a fixed amount." It does not. The result depends on the image, the format and the setting. Frequently Asked Questions

Does cropping reduce file size? ▾
Often, because fewer pixels are saved, but the amount depends on the content and format. Cropping is meant to change composition, not to hit a file-size target.
Which is better for hitting a KB limit, resizing or compressing? ▾
Usually a mix. Reducing the dimensions gives the largest saving with the least visible damage. Lowering quality then fine-tunes the size.
Can I resize without changing the shape? ▾
Yes. Keep the aspect ratio locked, and both dimensions change by the same proportion.
