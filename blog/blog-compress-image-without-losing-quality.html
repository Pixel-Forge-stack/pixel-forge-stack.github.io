How to Compress Images Without Losing Quality

The real difference between lossy and lossless compression, and how to pick the right quality setting so your file shrinks without anyone noticing.

A single photo from a modern phone can easily be 5 or 8 megabytes. That's a lot for something you just want to send in an email or upload to a website. The good news is you can usually shrink an image to a fraction of its size with no visible difference, once you understand how compression actually works.

What Does Compressing an Image Actually Mean?

Compressing an image means reducing its file size, the amount of storage it takes up. That's different from resizing, which changes the width and height in pixels. You can compress an image without changing its dimensions at all.

Every photo file stores a huge amount of information describing the colour of each tiny pixel. Compression finds smart ways to store that information using less data, without having to redraw the whole photo from scratch.

Two Types of Compression

There are two completely different approaches, and understanding the difference explains why some compressed photos look great and others look rough.

TypeHow It WorksQuality Loss
LosslessRemoves only unnecessary data, keeps everything visibleNone
LossyRemoves some visual detail the eye barely noticesSmall, adjustable

PNG is lossless, which is why PNG files tend to run larger. JPG and WebP use lossy compression, which is why they can be so much smaller while still looking nearly identical to the original at the right settings.

The key idea: the human eye can't detect tiny colour differences as precisely as a computer can measure them. Lossy compression removes exactly the kind of detail your eyes were never going to notice anyway.

Why File Size Actually Matters

  • Website speed. Large images are usually the single biggest reason a page loads slowly.
  • Search ranking. Page speed is a ranking factor, so heavy images can quietly hurt how easily people find your site.
  • Storage space. Compressing thousands of photos can free up real storage without deleting anything.
  • Sending and sharing. Many apps limit attachment sizes, and a smaller file uses less of the other person's mobile data too.

How to Compress an Image, Step by Step

  1. Upload your image by clicking the upload area or dragging the file onto the page.
  2. Set quality to around 80% as a starting point. That's usually the sweet spot where the file shrinks a lot but nobody can tell the difference.
  3. Watch the file size preview if there is one, so you can see exactly how much smaller it's getting as you move the slider.
  4. Zoom in and compare the compressed version against the original, especially fine details like text or hair.
  5. Lower it further only if you need a smaller file and can accept a small visible change.
  6. Download the compressed image. It's ready to upload, send, or publish.

Tips to Get the Best Results

Resize Before You Compress

If your photo is much larger in pixels than you actually need, resize it down first, then compress. A photo that's already the right size compresses far better than a huge one squeezed down with quality settings alone.

Choose the Right Format

JPG works well for photographs with lots of colour and gradients. PNG suits graphics, logos, or anything with sharp edges and text, since lossy compression can make text look fuzzy. WebP often beats both in file size while keeping similar or better quality.

Don't Compress the Same File Repeatedly

Every time you save a JPG, a small amount of quality is lost. Compress an already-compressed JPG again and again, and the loss adds up noticeably. Always compress from your original source file when you can.

Common Mistakes People Make

Compressing a screenshot or graphic as JPG out of habit. Text, flat colours, and sharp UI edges are exactly what JPG compression handles worst. A screenshot saved as JPG often looks noticeably softer around text than the same screenshot saved as PNG. For graphics and screenshots, PNG or lossless WebP is almost always the better call.

Assuming lower quality always means a smaller, better file. Dropping the quality slider very low does shrink the file further, but past a certain point the savings get small while the visible damage keeps growing. The 75 to 85 percent range usually gives the best trade-off, and going much lower rarely saves enough to be worth the quality loss.

Compressing before resizing. If an image is much larger in pixels than it needs to be, compressing it first still leaves you with a bigger file than necessary. Resize down to the size you actually need first, then compress, for the smallest realistic file size.

Overwriting the only copy of an important photo. Compression is a one-way process. Once detail is discarded, it can't be brought back. Keep your original, uncompressed photo saved somewhere safe, and only ever compress a copy of it.

A Simple Way to Remember It

Think of lossless compression like folding a letter neatly to fit it in a smaller envelope, nothing inside is changed, it's just packed more efficiently. Think of lossy compression like summarizing that same letter in fewer words, you lose a little of the original detail, but the meaning still comes through clearly if you don't overdo it. Most everyday images are perfectly happy being "summarized" a little. Only reach for lossless when every last detail truly needs to survive.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best quality setting to compress without losing quality? ▾
A setting between 75 and 85 percent is the sweet spot for most photos. File size typically drops 60 to 80 percent compared to the original, and the visual difference is nearly impossible to spot. Below 50 percent is where blurring or blocky artifacts usually start to show.
Does compressing an image change its dimensions? ▾
No, compressing only affects file size, not width and height. A compressed photo can be exactly the same dimensions as the original. Changing pixel dimensions is a separate step called resizing.
Which format compresses best without losing quality? ▾
WebP generally offers the best balance, producing smaller files than JPG at similar quality, and it supports both lossy and lossless modes. JPG remains the most universally compatible choice for photos. PNG is best reserved for images needing transparency or sharp text.
Can I compress a PNG without losing its transparent background? ▾
Yes, as long as the output stays PNG or WebP, both of which support transparency. Converting to JPG will fill transparent areas with a solid colour, usually white, since JPG doesn't support transparency at all.
