HEIC vs JPG: Differences, Compatibility and Converting

Why iPhone photos sometimes will not open elsewhere, and what changes when you convert them.

iPhones and iPads save photos as HEIC by default. JPG is older and far more widely supported. If you have ever sent an iPhone photo to a Windows computer, tried to upload it to a form, or opened it in an older program and seen an error, you have already met this difference.

This guide explains what each format is, how they compare on file size, quality and compatibility, why Apple chose HEIC, what really changes when you convert a HEIC file to JPG, and how to avoid the problem in the future. Everything is explained in simple words, with practical steps you can follow right away.

What Is HEIC?

HEIC stands for High Efficiency Image Container. It belongs to the HEIF family (High Efficiency Image File Format), a modern standard made for storing photos in smaller files. The pictures inside are usually compressed with HEVC, the same efficient method used for modern video. Apple made it the default photo format on iPhones and iPads with iOS 11 in 2017, so many people use it without ever choosing it.

HEIC is more than a plain picture format. One HEIC file can hold several images, depth information from Portrait mode, the frames of a Live Photo, and edit instructions. That is why it is flexible, but it is also why other programs sometimes do not know what to do with it.

What Is JPG?

JPG, also written JPEG, is one of the oldest and most common image formats. It was created in the early 1990s, and it uses lossy compression: it throws away small details the eye barely notices to make the file smaller. A JPG stores 8 bits for each colour channel, which gives about 16.7 million colours. Almost every phone, computer, browser, printer, website and app understands it. This is why JPG is the safe choice when you share a photo with someone else.

File Size: Why HEIC Is Smaller

HEIC uses a newer and smarter compression method, so it can keep similar visual quality in a much smaller file. In everyday use, a HEIC photo is often about 40 to 50 percent smaller than a JPG of similar quality. The exact numbers depend on the photo and the camera settings.

Here is a simple example. If a typical iPhone photo is about 2 MB as HEIC and about 4 MB as JPG, then 1000 photos take roughly 2 GB as HEIC and roughly 4 GB as JPG. On a phone with limited storage, that saving is why Apple chose HEIC.

The size difference also matters for uploads. A converted JPG is often larger than the original HEIC, so a form with a small KB limit may still reject it. If that happens, our guide on how to reduce image size in KB for forms shows what to do.

Image Quality: Is HEIC Better?

Both formats are usually lossy, and for normal viewing on a phone or screen most people cannot tell them apart. The differences appear in a few special situations:

  • Same file size. At the same size, HEIC usually keeps more detail and shows fewer blocky patches than JPG.
  • Smooth gradients. Skies and skin tones can show faint bands in JPG because it has fewer colour steps. HEIC can store more colour steps.
  • Editing. Every time you edit and save a JPG, it is compressed again and loses a little more detail. HEIC can keep edit instructions, so changes can be undone.
  • Wide colour and HDR. HEIC can carry wider colour and HDR data. A standard JPG keeps less of it.

So in a technical sense HEIC is the better format. In daily life the better format is the one that opens everywhere, and that is where JPG still wins.

Why Compatibility Differs

HEIC support depends on the operating system, the app and sometimes extra codecs. Apple devices handle it well. Windows may need extra extensions, and many websites, forms and older programs do not accept it. JPG is accepted almost everywhere you upload a photo, which is why people convert.

What Changes When You Convert to JPG

  • Quality: JPG is lossy, so the photo is encoded again. At high quality the difference is small, but it is not an exact copy.
  • File size: the JPG is often larger than the HEIC. Use Compress Image or Resize Image if you need it smaller.
  • Metadata: the HEIC to JPG tool redraws the photo on a canvas, so details such as EXIF and location are normally not carried over.
  • Extras: things stored alongside the picture in HEIC, such as depth data, are not included in the JPG.

Two more things are worth knowing. First, a Live Photo or a burst becomes one still image, because JPG holds only a single picture. Second, wide colour photos can look slightly less vivid after conversion, because JPG usually uses the standard colour range.

Does converting reduce quality?

Slightly, because the photo is compressed again. At a high quality setting you will almost never notice it. The real damage happens if you convert again and again, or if you use a very low quality setting. Convert once, from the original, and keep the original file.

Using the Converter

The HEIC to JPG tool relies on your browser to open the HEIC file, so it only works where the browser can decode HEIC. Safari on an iPhone or Mac generally can. If your browser cannot, you will see an error instead of a download.

  1. Open the HEIC to JPG tool in Safari on your iPhone or Mac, or in another browser that can decode HEIC.
  2. Choose your HEIC photo from your device.
  3. Wait for the conversion, then check that the picture looks right.
  4. Download the JPG and keep the original HEIC safe.
  5. Reduce the size if needed. If a form has a small limit, run the JPG through the compress tool.

If the Converter Will Not Open Your File

  • The browser cannot read HEIC. Try Safari on an Apple device. Chrome, Firefox and Edge on some computers cannot decode HEIC by themselves.
  • The file is only renamed. A file renamed from .jpg to .heic, or the other way, is not a real conversion. The converter needs a real HEIC picture.
  • The file did not fully download. If the photo is stored in iCloud, download the original to your device first.
  • Use another device. Convert on your iPhone or Mac, then send the JPG to your computer.
  • Change the camera format. Set the iPhone to take JPG photos from now on, as shown below.

How to Avoid Conversions in the Future

To avoid conversions in future, an iPhone can save new photos as JPG: open Settings, then Camera, then Formats, and choose Most Compatible. The trade-off is larger files and the loss of HEIC's space savings.

If you move photos to a Windows computer with a cable, there is a second setting. In the iPhone Photos settings, under Transfer to Mac or PC, choose Automatic. The iPhone then changes photos to a compatible format during the transfer, and Keep Originals leaves them as HEIC.

Opening HEIC without converting

You do not always have to convert. On a Mac, Preview and Photos open HEIC directly. On Windows, the Photos app can open it after you install the HEIF image extension from the Microsoft Store, and some computers also need an HEVC extension, which may be free or paid depending on the device. Google Photos on Android can open HEIC. Converting is still the best choice when you must send or upload the file.

Which Should You Keep?

Keep HEIC as the master if you stay in an Apple environment and want to save space. Use JPG for sharing, uploading to forms and anything that needs to open on many devices. Keep the original in either case before you convert. For a comparison with other web formats, see JPG vs PNG vs WebP.

Common Mistakes

Renaming .heic to .jpg. Changing the extension does not convert the picture. The file inside is still HEIC, and many programs will still fail to open it.

Converting the same photo again and again. Each round adds a little more loss. Convert once from the original.

Deleting the original HEIC. Once the original is gone, you cannot get back the depth data and extra details. Keep it.

Using unknown converters for private photos. Many websites upload your picture to a server. For personal photos, use a tool that works in your browser.

Forgetting the size limit. A converted JPG can be bigger than the HEIC. If the form has a KB limit, compress after converting.

Changing the camera format and forgetting. If you set the iPhone to Most Compatible, remember that your storage will fill faster.

Quick Checklist

  • Do I really need JPG, or can the other person or site open HEIC?
  • Have I kept the original HEIC file?
  • Did I convert only once, with a high quality setting?
  • Does the JPG look right when I open it?
  • Is the file within the size limit of the form or site?
  • Do I need the location details? If yes, do not rely on the converted JPG to keep them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is HEIC better than JPG? ▾
HEIC is generally more space-efficient at similar visual quality, but JPG is more widely supported. "Better" depends on whether you need compatibility or storage savings. For sharing and uploading, JPG is the safer choice. For saving space on an iPhone, HEIC is the better one.
Does converting HEIC to JPG lose quality? ▾
Slightly, because JPG is lossy. At a high quality setting the difference is usually small and hard to see. Avoid converting the same picture many times, and keep your original HEIC.
Why will the converter not open my HEIC file? ▾
The tool depends on your browser decoding HEIC. If it cannot, try Safari on an Apple device, or change the iPhone camera format. Also make sure the file is a real HEIC picture and that it is fully downloaded from iCloud.
Will the JPG keep my location data? ▾
Do not count on it. The conversion normally drops metadata such as EXIF and location. This can be handy for privacy, but if you need the date or place, keep the original HEIC.
Can I just rename a HEIC file to JPG? ▾
No. Renaming only changes the name, not the data inside. The picture is still HEIC, so many programs and websites will still refuse it. You need a real conversion with a converter tool.
How do I stop my iPhone from taking HEIC photos? ▾
Open Settings, then Camera, then Formats, and choose Most Compatible. New photos will be saved as JPG. The trade-off is larger files, so your storage will fill faster.
Why is my converted JPG bigger than the HEIC? ▾
HEIC compresses more efficiently, so the same picture usually takes less space than a JPG of similar quality. After converting, you can reduce the size with a compress or resize tool if a form has a size limit.
Can Windows or Android open HEIC files? ▾
Often yes, but it depends. Windows may need the HEIF image extension from the Microsoft Store. Newer Android versions and apps such as Google Photos can open HEIC, but older systems and many websites cannot. Converting to JPG is the easiest way to be sure.
