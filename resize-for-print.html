The Basics

Pixels, Inches and DPI

A digital image is a grid of pixels. Paper has a physical size. DPI (dots per inch, or more precisely PPI, pixels per inch) links the two: pixel dimensions divided by PPI gives the printed size in inches. A 1200 × 1800 pixel image printed at 300 PPI measures 4 × 6 inches.

That means a photo needs enough pixels for the size you want to print. A 12-megapixel phone photo (about 4000 × 3000 pixels) has more than enough for a 4 × 6 inch print at 300 PPI, but it would be stretched thin on A3.

DPI alone does not decide print quality. Sharpness, focus, noise, colour handling, paper and the printer all matter. And changing a DPI number without changing the pixel dimensions does not add detail.

How It Works

How This Tool Fits the Image

The tool creates a canvas at the output size, fills it with white, and draws your image inside it at the largest size that fits while keeping its proportions. It does not crop or stretch. If the image's ratio differs from the print format, white bars appear on two sides.

To fill the page completely, crop first with Crop Image to the format's ratio, for example 2:3 for 4 × 6 inches. JPG output is saved at 95% quality. PNG is lossless, with larger files. For a single A4 preset that stretches instead of fitting, see Resize to A4.

Choosing a Resolution

When 300 PPI Is and Is Not Needed

Photo prints and documents read at arm's length commonly use 300 PPI.Large posters and banners are viewed from farther away, so lower resolutions such as 150 PPI or less are often acceptable. Print shops usually publish their own recommendations.Fine line art and small text can benefit from higher resolutions such as 600 PPI.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is 300 DPI so common? ▾
At typical reading distance, 300 pixels per inch is dense enough that individual pixels are hard to see in most photo prints, so it became a common target. It is a rule of thumb, not a rule.
Can I print at less than 300 DPI? ▾
Yes. Large prints such as posters are usually viewed from farther away and often use lower resolutions. A lower value will show less fine detail, particularly close up.
Does a higher DPI value make the print better? ▾
Not by itself. DPI only describes how many pixels are placed per inch. If the source photo does not contain enough detail, saving it at a larger size or higher DPI cannot add any.
Why does my image have white borders? ▾
The tool fits the whole image inside the chosen size without cropping or stretching, so a different aspect ratio leaves white margins. Crop the image to the print proportions first if you want it to fill the paper.
Does the file store the DPI value? ▾
No. The tool sets the pixel dimensions only. The printed size depends on how you place the image on the page or the scaling you choose in the print dialog.
Is this enough to make a valid passport photo? ▾
No. It only sets the pixel size. Photo rules for background, head size and file type are set by the issuing authority, so check them separately.
