Concepts

Lossy and Lossless Compression

Lossy compression (JPG, and WebP in lossy mode) makes files smaller by discarding detail that is least likely to be noticed. Lower quality settings discard more, and visible artifacts such as blockiness or smeared edges eventually appear. Repeated lossy saves add up, so compress from the original when you can.Lossless compression (PNG, and WebP in lossless mode) stores the same pixels in fewer bytes, so nothing is lost, but the savings are usually smaller, especially for photographs.

Results vary a lot. The same quality setting can shrink one image dramatically and another only slightly, depending on its content, its dimensions, the original format and the encoder used. Treat any percentage you read elsewhere as an example, not a promise.

Formats

JPG, PNG or WebP?

JPG suits photographs and is widely supported. It has no transparency.PNG suits screenshots, logos and graphics with flat colours or text, and supports transparency. Photographs saved as PNG are usually large.WebP supports lossy and lossless modes and transparency, and often produces smaller files than JPG at similar quality. It is well supported by current browsers but less so by older software, and the ability to save WebP in this tool depends on your browser. See JPG vs PNG vs WebP for a longer comparison.
Dimensions and File Size

Why Resizing First Often Helps

File size depends on how many pixels an image has as well as on the quality setting. A 4000 × 3000 phone photo has far more pixels than a picture that will be shown 800 pixels wide. Reducing the dimensions often saves more than lowering quality, and with less visible damage.

Example: a form asks for 100 KB or less.
  1. Resize the image to dimensions that suit its purpose, using Resize Image.
  2. Export as JPG or WebP if you do not need transparency.
  3. Lower the quality gradually and check the measured size.
  4. Stop as soon as the file is under the limit, so you do not lose more quality than needed.

Many forms count a kilobyte as 1000 bytes, while this tool shows 1 KB as 1024 bytes. Leave a little margin below the limit. There are dedicated pages for 100 KB, 150 KB, 200 KB, 1 MB and 2 MB, and a guide to reducing image size for forms.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the "After" size shown by the tool accurate? ▾
Yes. The tool encodes the image in your browser at the current quality and format and shows the real size of that file, so it matches what you download. It updates a moment after you move the slider.
What quality setting should I use? ▾
There is no single best value. Around 75 to 85% is a reasonable starting point for many photographs, but the result depends on the image, its dimensions and the format. Lower the slider until the file is small enough and the picture still looks acceptable at full size.
Why does the slider not change the size of my PNG? ▾
PNG is a lossless format, so browsers ignore the quality setting when saving PNG. To reduce a PNG, resize it, or save it as JPG or WebP if you do not need transparency or exact pixels.
Can I reach an exact size such as 100 KB? ▾
You can get under a limit by lowering quality and by reducing the dimensions first. The tool does not hit an exact target automatically, so adjust the slider and watch the measured size. The pages for 100 KB, 150 KB, 200 KB, 1 MB and 2 MB show whether you are under each limit.
Will transparent areas survive? ▾
Only with PNG or WebP output. JPG cannot store transparency, so the tool fills transparent areas with white before saving as JPG.
Does this tool resize the image? ▾
No, it keeps the original pixel dimensions and changes only the encoding. If the file is still too large at an acceptable quality, resize it first with Resize Image.
